## Painting earns Meta's place on this site

Most of the trades this site covers are found, not created. Nobody wakes up and decides today is the day for a switchboard upgrade — the switchboard fails, or an inspection flags it, and the homeowner searches for someone to fix it. That is demand you capture, not demand you make, and search advertising suits it because the person is already typing the problem into Google.

Painting sits differently. A full exterior repaint is almost always discretionary, and it can sit on a homeowner's mental list for a year without anything forcing the decision. The house is not broken. It is just tired — flaking on the north-facing weatherboards, a colour that stopped feeling right, joinery that could do with attention before it needs more than paint. Nothing about that is urgent, which means nobody is reliably searching for it on any given day.

That is exactly the gap a photo can close. A sharp before-and-after of a similar villa, a similar street, a similar problem — shown to someone who was not looking, at a moment they were only scrolling — can turn a vague year-long intention into a filled-in form in the space of thirty seconds. That is interruption working as intended, and it is the opposite of how a burst pipe or a tripped switchboard gets found. For a trade this visual, Meta is not a consolation prize next to search. It is doing a job search cannot: creating the moment of decision, not waiting for someone to arrive at it on their own.

None of that replaces search. Someone who has already decided to repaint and is comparing painters by typing the words into Google is a different, more qualified enquiry, and worth capturing on its own terms — reasoned through properly in the [paid campaigns overview](/campaigns/). The point for painting specifically is that Meta genuinely earns a place in the mix here in a way it would not for a trade with no visual or discretionary angle to work with.

## What a painting ad enquiry actually looks like

An enquiry that arrives off a painting ad tends to share a few traits worth planning around.

**The job value is usually meaningful, and the decision is not instant.** A full exterior repaint is one of the larger jobs a homeowner will book all year. Nobody signs off on that from a form fill alone — they want a measure-up, a proper quote, and usually a look at what else you have done. The ad's job is to start that conversation, not finish it.

**The customer is very likely getting more than one quote.** Painting attracts multiple-quote comparison more reliably than almost any other trade on this site — it is visual and easy to picture from the outside, so getting three numbers on a bench feels like ordinary due diligence rather than overkill. An ad enquiry is not usually a customer choosing you. It is a customer adding you to a shortlist, and [what actually decides which quote wins](/guides/painting-quotes-against-competition/) once that shortlist exists is worth reading alongside this, because responsiveness is the first visible difference between the three of you, long before any paint goes on.

**The interest can be genuinely impulsive.** This is the trait that makes painting different from almost everything else on this site, and it deserves its own section below.

## The speed problem is specific here, and it is worse than it looks

Most trades lose a slow-answered enquiry to a competitor. Painting has an extra failure mode on top of that, and it is worse because you never see it happen.

Think about who actually fills in a form after a scroll-stopping ad. They were not planning to contact a painter that day — they were looking at photos of dogs, or the news, or a mate's holiday, and a transformation photo caught them mid-scroll. Something about it landed: their own house looks a bit like that one did, or the colour is one they have been half-thinking about. They fill in the form on the spot, while the feeling is fresh.

That feeling has a short half-life. It was not a plan; it was an impulse, and impulses cool. By the next morning, the person who filled in that form may not clearly remember which painter's ad it was, may have half-forgotten they did it at all, or may simply have moved on to the next thing competing for their attention. If your reply lands the same day, you are talking to someone who still remembers wanting this. If it lands two or three days later, you may be talking to someone who has to be reminded why they filled in a form in the first place — assuming they reply at all.

This is not the same failure as losing a job to a competitor who rang back faster. There, at least, you would eventually notice the pattern — quotes not converting, jobs going quiet. Here, there is no rival to blame and often no signal at all. The enquiry does not go anywhere. It just stops. You paid for the click, you paid for the impression that stopped the scroll, and the interest it created evaporated before you ever got to it. That is a cost with no visible cause, which is exactly why it is easy to miss and easy to keep paying for without noticing.

The [pillar guide on reply speed](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) sets out realistic targets and what the wider research on response time actually shows, with the caveats that come with it. The point specific to painting is narrower: an enquiry born from a scroll-stopping photo is more fragile than one born from a homeowner actively hunting for a painter, because nothing forced the decision in the first place. Nothing has to keep it alive either, except a reply that arrives while the impulse is still warm.

## Being easy to deal with is the differentiator, not the price

Because painting attracts a shortlist rather than a single decision, the honest question is not "how do we win against the other painters" but "what does the customer actually have to go on, this early, before any of the three of you have picked up a brush." At the enquiry stage, they cannot judge prep work, coat counts or finish quality — none of that exists yet. What they can judge is how each of the three businesses handled the first contact: who replied, how fast, and how clearly.

That is not a case for cutting your price to win the shortlist faster. Undercutting the other two numbers trains a customer to keep collecting quotes and pick on price, which is the exact dynamic that makes painting feel like a commodity from the outside. Being straightforward and quick to deal with is a genuine point of difference precisely because it costs nothing to compete on and says something true about what working with you will actually be like — which is a fair thing for a customer to weigh, and a reasonable thing for a painting business to be proud of.

## Seasonality is not optional to plan around

Exterior painting in New Zealand is weather-dependent in a way that interior work is not, and campaign spend that ignores this buys enquiries for work that physically cannot start. Running full exterior campaigns hard through the middle of winter, when a job booked today might not have a realistic start date for months, means holding a warm enquiry for longer than the impulse that generated it was ever going to survive — the exact problem the section above describes, just stretched out further. It also means a customer waiting through a gap wide enough for another painter's ad to catch them first.

The straightforward answer is to weight spend toward what the season can actually deliver: exterior campaigns building through late winter and spring toward the settled months, interior work carrying more of the load when the weather will not cooperate. None of this needs to be exact — nobody can predict a New Zealand spring — but running exterior ads at full pace into a forecast that will not let the job start is spending against the season rather than with it.

## The order this has to happen in

None of the above matters if the enquiry an ad generates sits unanswered. This site's whole position on paid campaigns is that they come second: the front desk gets built first, so a fast enquiry — cooling by the hour, comparing you against two other quotes — actually gets caught. Running ads before that is in place means paying, per enquiry, to generate exactly the kind of fragile interest most likely to be lost to a slow reply. [What running campaigns looks like once you are ready for them](/campaigns/) covers that order properly, including what is and is not included and how the money works. For a painting business specifically, the case for getting the order right is sharper than most — because the enquiry a good ad creates is the easiest kind of lead this site talks about to lose without ever finding out it happened.
