Every after-hours call to an electrician arrives sounding the same: someone frightened, on their phone in a dark house, sure it can't wait until Monday. Some of them are right. A house with no power, a smell of burning, a switchboard that's sparking, a freezer full of stock going warm — those are calls that need action tonight. Others are a tripped RCD the customer has already reset twice, or a switch that's been faulty for a fortnight and has simply become tonight's problem because everyone's home and irritated. The caller usually can't tell you which one it is, because they don't have the training to know, and fear doesn't sound different to genuine danger over the phone. That's the actual problem after-hours calls create for an electrician — not the electrical fault itself, but working out, fast, from a frightened voice or a three-line text, what kind of call you're holding.

## Two calls that sound identical

This is what makes electrical after-hours work harder to triage than most other trades. A blocked drain at 9pm is unpleasant but rarely dangerous, so plumbers get some slack to make that call in the morning. An electrical fault carries a chance of being genuinely serious — but most after-hours electrical calls are not that. The problem is that "no power in half the house" and "no power in half the house because something is properly wrong" produce the exact same sentence from the customer. You are not being handed a description of the fault. You are being handed a description of how someone feels about the fault, filtered through a phone call at the end of a long day, and it's the electrician who has to work out what it actually is — not the person calling, and not us.

Get that read wrong in one direction and you're driving forty minutes for a job that would have kept until 8am. Get it wrong in the other and a real hazard sits overnight, and the customer who called you remembers, next time, that nobody came — and rings someone else.

## Why guessing at 9pm doesn't work

The moment a fault call comes in is the worst possible moment to be inventing a decision process from scratch. You're off the tools, probably mid-dinner or already in bed, and the caller's fear is doing a lot of the talking. Under those conditions it's easy to either say yes to everything — because saying no to a genuine emergency is the outcome nobody wants to risk — or to get jaded from too many false alarms and start saying no to things you shouldn't. Neither is a considered decision. Both are a coin flip dressed up as judgement.

The businesses that handle this well aren't the ones with better instincts at 9pm. They're the ones who did the thinking earlier, in daylight, when nobody was frightened and nothing was on fire. What counts as an immediate callout for your business, what can hold until morning, who gets called if it's genuinely urgent — that's a set of rules worth having settled before the phone rings, not while it's ringing.

## What the call itself needs to do

None of this is about assessing the fault — that stays entirely with the electrician, every time, and nothing about how a call is captured should pretend otherwise. What the call can do is collect what the customer is actually reporting, in their own words, consistently, so that whoever looks at it — you, at 9.45pm, deciding whether to get dressed — is working from a clear account rather than a rushed voicemail with half the details missing. A name, an address, a number, and what they're seeing or hearing right now. That's the raw material. Sorting it against your own rules for what's urgent is the electrician's decision, made from facts instead of adrenaline.

This is also where consistency matters more than speed. A caller who gets three different questions depending on who happens to answer — or gets no questions at all, just a rushed "I'll call you back" — is harder to triage accurately than one who's asked the same short set every time. The value isn't in asking more. It's in asking the same thing, every time, so the answers can actually be compared and acted on.

## Capture first, decide second

The other failure mode is simpler and has nothing to do with judgement at all: the call just doesn't get captured properly. It rings out to voicemail nobody checks until Monday. A text arrives and sits unread. By the time anyone sees it, the moment where a same-night callout would have mattered has passed either way — and so has the moment where you could have reassured a nervous customer that a tripped switch really can wait. [After-hours cover](/after-hours-cover/) covers the mechanics of getting after-hours enquiries captured and sorted by rules you set, rather than left ringing out or landing in an inbox nobody's watching until the tools go back on.

For [electricians](/electricians/) specifically, the stakes on getting this right are higher than for most trades, because the range between "trivial" and "genuinely dangerous" is wider than it is for a lot of other after-hours callouts, and because that range is invisible from outside the job. A blocked drain rarely masquerades as an emergency. A dead board sometimes does, and sometimes isn't one at all.

## Build the rule once, not every night

The businesses that handle after-hours fault calls well aren't necessarily answering more of them personally. They've decided, ahead of time and without a frightened customer on the line, what an urgent callout looks like for their business, who gets alerted when one comes in, and what happens to everything else — a callback booked for first thing, not a promise made at midnight and forgotten by 8am. That rule does the hard part once, so that at 9pm the only thing left to do is apply it to what the caller has actually reported.

This is really a sharper version of a problem every trade business has: an enquiry that arrives outside business hours needs to be captured, understood, and moved somewhere useful before the caller gives up and rings the next name on their list. [Handling trade enquiries](/guides/handling-trade-enquiries/) covers that process end to end — capture, respond, qualify, book, follow up. For an electrician, the qualifying step at 9pm carries more weight than it does for most, because the cost of getting it wrong runs in both directions: a wasted trip, or a hazard left till morning. Neither is a risk worth leaving to guesswork.
