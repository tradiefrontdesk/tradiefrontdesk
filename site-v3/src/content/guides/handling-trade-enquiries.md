Handling trade enquiries well comes down to five things, done in order: capture every enquiry so none go missing, reply fast, qualify before you quote, book while the customer is still keen, and follow up until you get a clear yes or no. Most trade businesses don't lose work because of bad pricing or bad workmanship. They lose it because an enquiry sat unanswered for a day, or went quiet after a quote and nobody chased it. Get those five stages right and you win more of the jobs you are already being asked about, without changing anything about how you price or how you work.

**The five stages:**

1. **Capture** — every call, text, form and message lands somewhere you can see it, including the ones you miss.
2. **Respond** — someone acknowledges it before the customer rings the next name on their list.
3. **Qualify** — you find out what the job is, where, and when, before you spend time quoting it.
4. **Book** — it goes on the calendar, or the quote goes out, while the customer is still engaged.
5. **Follow up** — you chase on a schedule instead of leaving it to the customer to come back to you.

## Capture: don't let the enquiry disappear before you see it

You cannot respond to, qualify, or follow up on an enquiry you never knew existed. Capture is what everything else depends on, and it is the stage most tradies get wrong without noticing, because a missed call doesn't feel like a lost job. It feels like nothing happened.

For a one or two-person outfit, enquiries arrive through whatever channel the customer picked: a call while you are up a ladder, a text to your personal mobile, a form on your website, a message through Facebook or a Google listing. If those are not all landing somewhere you actually check, some simply vanish. No missed call gets returned. No form gets read until Friday. The customer, meanwhile, has usually rung two or three others in the same trade.

This is structural, not a personal failing. Stats NZ puts the number of self-employed contractors in New Zealand at around 144,000 — roughly one in twenty employed people — and a large share are running a trade business with no office support at all. When you are the estimator, the labourer and the receptionist in the same body, capture loses the most, because there is no second person catching what you miss while your hands are full.

The specific mechanism worth understanding here is missed-call text-back. If you have heard the term and are not sure what it does, [our explainer covers it plainly](/guides/what-is-missed-call-text-back/). Capture does not have to be sophisticated — a notebook by the phone works, provided it is actually used. What matters is that nothing arrives and then quietly disappears.

## Respond: speed decides more jobs than price does

Once an enquiry is captured, the clock starts. This is where businesses lose jobs to competitors who are not better or cheaper, just faster to answer.

There is no independent research specific to New Zealand trades on how much reply speed matters, so treat what follows as general context rather than a claim about your business. A 2011 *Harvard Business Review* study by Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington audited 2,241 US firms responding to genuine enquiries and found the average first response took 42 hours, 23% never replied at all, and firms responding within an hour were substantially more likely to make contact than those that waited a day or more. That is a US, largely office-based sample — not tradies, not New Zealand. But the pattern lines up with what most people in the trades already suspect: a customer ringing around for a quote usually keeps ringing until someone picks up.

Responding does not mean answering live. A text, an acknowledgement, or a callback booked for a specific time all count, because they tell the customer someone is coming. [How fast you actually need to reply](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) goes into realistic targets for someone on the tools all day. Where this breaks down most is missed calls that never get any reply at all — see [missed calls](/missed-calls/) if that is a regular problem.

## Qualify: work out whether it's a job you want

Not every enquiry is worth quoting. Some are out of area, out of scope, or a mismatch for what you do, and working that out before you spend twenty minutes on a detailed quote saves time for everyone, including the customer.

Qualifying means asking a short, consistent set of questions before committing to a site visit: what the job is, where it is, when they need it, and whether anything about access, scope or budget would change whether you take it. The channel matters less than doing it every time rather than only when you remember.

Skip it and you end up quoting jobs you never wanted, or turning up to a "quick job" that is nothing of the sort. [Qualifying enquiries](/qualifying-enquiries/) covers the questions worth asking and the ones worth skipping.

## Book: turn a good enquiry into a job on the calendar

If the enquiry is a fit, move it to something concrete — a booked site visit, a scheduled callout, or a quote with a clear next step — while the customer is still engaged.

The failure here is simple: a customer is ready to book, the tradie says "I'll call you back to confirm a time", and then doesn't, because the next job gets in the way. If you can offer a time on the spot, even a rough window, you remove the gap where their attention moves elsewhere.

It does not matter whether the calendar is a paper diary on the dash or a shared one on your phone. What matters is that one version exists, everyone who needs it can see it, and a booked slot actually gets held. Businesses that struggle here are usually not missing a tool. They are missing the ten minutes at smoko to keep the one they have up to date.

## Follow up: most jobs are won after the first quote

This is the stage that gets skipped most, and it has the most jobs sitting in it. A quote goes out, you move on, and unless the customer chases, nothing happens. The assumption — reasonably enough — is that if they wanted it, they would come back.

In practice customers go quiet for reasons that have nothing to do with your price. They are comparing two or three quotes. They got busy. They are waiting on a partner, a body corporate, or an insurer. None of that is a no. It means nobody has asked again. [Why customers go quiet](/guides/why-customers-go-quiet/) goes through the reasons, because which one it is changes what a useful follow-up looks like.

A workable approach is to treat follow-up as a scheduled step attached to every quote rather than something you do if you remember: one a few days after it goes out, a second about a week later if there is still no answer, then leave it. [Quote follow-up](/quote-follow-up/) covers what running that consistently looks like.

## Where enquiries actually get lost

In roughly the order they cost the most jobs:

- **After-hours enquiries.** A burst pipe at 8pm doesn't wait for business hours, and if nothing captures it until Monday the customer has already rung someone else. [After-hours cover](/after-hours-cover/) looks at handling this without being on call every night.
- **Calls during a job.** The phone is in the ute, or on silent, and there is no way for that call to be captured and returned later.
- **Quotes that go out and get forgotten** by both sides, because neither had a next step scheduled.
- **Enquiries with no record.** If a customer's number only exists in your recent calls list, there is nothing to review at the end of the week.
- **Busy periods.** A run of big jobs means smaller enquiries get answered last, or not at all.

None of these are about being bad at the trade. They are about a business built around doing the work well, where the front-of-house side has no dedicated time set aside for it.

## Doing it yourself, hiring, or outsourcing

**Doing it yourself** costs nothing extra and keeps you in control of every conversation, but it competes directly with time on the tools. It works fine at low volume and gets harder as you grow.

**Hiring** gives you someone who knows the business, but it is a real employment commitment — wages, leave, training — whether enquiries are flowing or the phone has gone quiet for a fortnight.

**Outsourcing** sits between the two: a fixed monthly cost with no employment overhead, but you are handing the first conversation to someone outside the business, which only works if they have clear instructions on how you price, what jobs you take, and what promises you are comfortable making.

There is a fourth option worth naming honestly: change nothing and accept the enquiries you lose as a cost of running the business the way you always have. That is legitimate at low volume. It is only worth changing if what is slipping through adds up to more than the fix would cost.

## What good looks like

None of this requires buying anything. It is the standard worth measuring against whichever option you are using:

- Every call, text and form lands somewhere you can see later — a shared notebook beats memory.
- You can look at any point in the day and see which of today's enquiries have had no reply.
- New enquiries get at least an acknowledgement the same day.
- You ask the same qualifying questions every time, before committing to a quote or site visit.
- Every quote that goes out has a follow-up date attached, not an open-ended "they'll come back if they want it".
- Missed and after-hours enquiries get some response, even just confirming you have the message.
- Once a week you look back at which enquiries went cold, and roughly why.

Do all seven consistently and the enquiry side of the business is in reasonable shape regardless of how you have staffed it.

## When a managed front desk makes sense, and when it doesn't

It makes sense when enquiry volume is unpredictable rather than a handful a month, when you are holding both the tools and the phone with no spare capacity for either, when after-hours enquiries are common in your trade, or when quotes are going out and not being chased and that is costing more than a monthly service would.

It does not make sense at low, steady volume you can comfortably keep up with, if you are booked out for months and not chasing new work, or if you want every enquiry to go through you personally. A managed front desk answers, qualifies and books. It is not meant to replace your judgement on pricing, scope, or which jobs you take — that stays with you.

## The bottom line

Enquiry handling is not one problem. It is five smaller ones stacked together, and a business can be strong at some and weak at others. A tradie who replies fast but never follows up is leaking jobs somewhere different to one who follows up religiously but misses half their after-hours calls. Working out which stage is actually costing you is worth more than guessing at a fix.
