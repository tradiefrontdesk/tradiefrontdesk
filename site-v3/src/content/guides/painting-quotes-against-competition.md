## Three quotes on the bench

Ask a painter what job type brings in the most competing quotes and most will say the same thing without hesitating: painting itself. A homeowner getting a switchboard upgraded rarely gets three quotes — they do not know what a good one looks like, so they tend to go with whoever an electrician mate recommended. A full exterior repaint is different. Anyone can look at a house and picture it a different colour. Anyone can read three numbers on three pieces of paper and pick the smallest one. Painting is the trade that looks, from the customer's side, like a commodity — same paint, same walls, same result, just three different prices for it.

That is not actually true, and every painter reading this knows it. Two coats over a proper sand and a stain-blocking primer is a different job to one coat over a quick scuff. A painter who preps the joinery properly and works to a schedule is doing a different job to one who does not — even if the finished wall looks identical in a photo six weeks later. But the customer comparing three quotes cannot see any of that. They see three numbers, maybe a line or two of what is included, and nothing that tells them which painter actually turns up when they say they will.

This is the real problem with quoting for painting work. It is not that you are underpriced or overpriced. Your quote is being read next to two others by someone with no way to judge the thing that actually matters — and price is the only variable they can see clearly.

## What the customer cannot compare

Walk through what a homeowner is actually holding when three painting quotes land on the kitchen table. Three totals. Maybe a line or two describing the job — "exterior repaint, two coats" against "full exterior including trim, two coats plus primer where needed." Even where one painter has itemised prep work and another has not, most homeowners do not know that a coat of stain-blocking primer on weatherboard joints is doing real work, not padding the bill.

What they cannot see from three pieces of paper:

- Whether "two coats" means two coats over bare, sanded timber or two coats slapped over what is already there.
- Whether the quote includes filling and sanding gib cracks properly or skims past them.
- Whether the painter who quoted lowest is planning to spend three days on your house or one and a half.
- Which of the three will actually show up on the date they said, and which will go quiet the moment a bigger job comes along.

None of that is visible in a number. It only becomes visible once the job is underway — by which point the homeowner has already chosen, mostly on price, because price was the only thing they had enough information to compare.

This is the frustrating part for a painter who does the work properly. The quality difference is real, and it is almost completely invisible at the point the decision gets made.

## The one thing that is visible before the paint goes on

There is exactly one part of the job a homeowner can judge before any of the three painters has done a stroke of work: how each of them handled the enquiry.

Did they call back the same day, or did the message sit for three days? Did they show up to measure when they said they would, or turn up forty minutes late without a text? Did the quote read like it understood the actual job — the flaking weatherboards on the north side, the extra coat the gib patch will need — or like a generic price dropped into a template?

None of that tells the homeowner anything about paint quality. All of it tells them what it will be like dealing with this person for the next two weeks. Because painting genuinely does look interchangeable from the outside, that impression carries more weight than it would for a trade where the customer already has some way to judge the work itself.

This starts earlier than most painters think — with the first phone call, not the quote document. A homeowner ringing three painters on the same Saturday morning forms an opinion about each of them in the first thirty seconds: who picked up, who sounded rushed, who asked sensible questions about the job versus who just asked for the address. By the time the written quotes arrive, two of the three decisions may already be half made.

## What this means for the quote itself

None of this is an argument for cutting your price to win the comparison. Undercutting the other two numbers on the bench trains the customer to keep collecting quotes and pick the cheapest, which is exactly the dynamic that makes painting feel like a commodity in the first place. It also tells the homeowner nothing about why your price is what it is.

The more useful move is to make the invisible parts of the job visible, briefly, inside the quote itself. Not a lecture on primer chemistry — a homeowner does not need to know the difference between an acrylic and an oil-based undercoat. But a line that says the weatherboards need a scrape and sand before painting, not just a repaint, tells them something real. A note that the two coats specified are two full coats over a primed surface, not two over whatever is already there, gives them a reason the number is what it is. It does not need to be long. It needs to exist, because the other two quotes on the bench probably will not have it.

The rest of the difference gets made before the quote is even written — in how fast the enquiry gets a reply, how clearly the measure-up gets booked, and whether the customer is left wondering if they have been forgotten. ## Staying in the running after the quote goes in

A full exterior repaint is not a same-day decision. The customer is waiting to line up three numbers, possibly waiting on a partner to agree, possibly waiting for the other two painters to actually turn up and measure. That gap — sometimes a week, sometimes closer to a month for a bigger job — is where quotes quietly die even when the painter would have won the job.

This is not the place to repeat how quote follow-up works in general; that is covered on [quote follow-up for tradies](/quote-follow-up/), and the mechanics — how soon, how many times, what to say — are the same regardless of trade. What is specific to painting is what a check-in is competing against. You are not the only one who might follow up. If one of the other two painters rings to confirm the quote landed and you do not, that becomes one more visible difference on the side of the ledger the homeowner cannot see from a piece of paper.

Being easy to reach, clear about what is included, and organised enough to check in without being asked will not always beat a lower number. Some customers will always go with whoever is cheapest. But for the homeowner who genuinely cannot tell two coats from three, being the painter who was straightforward to deal with from the first call onward is often the only real difference they have to go on.
