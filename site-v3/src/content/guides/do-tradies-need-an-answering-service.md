Short answer: no, not automatically. Plenty of tradies run a business for years off nothing but their own phone and never lose meaningful work to it. If you are a solo operator, fully booked through repeat customers and referrals, and you get back to most calls the same day, an answering service solves a problem you do not have — and paying a few hundred a month for it would be dead money.

Where it earns its keep is different: businesses missing enough calls to matter, after-hours enquiries going nowhere, or growth outpacing what one person on the tools can also answer for.

Here is how to tell which one you are.

## Who genuinely doesn't need one

Saying this plainly, because most content in this space will not: **if any of these describe you, save your money.**

- **You're solo, fully booked, mostly on referral and repeat work.** Your customers know you, they text you directly, and they are not shopping you against three others. There is no leak to fix.
- **You genuinely return most calls the same day.** If your habit is solid, a paid service fixes something that is not costing you anything.
- **Your call volume fits in your head.** A handful of calls a week is a mental note, not a system problem.
- **You're not trying to grow past what you can personally handle.** If staying a one-person operation is the plan, more infrastructure is just more cost and more to manage.

None of that means ignoring missed calls. A five-minute callback habit and a decent voicemail greeting cost nothing and fix most of what a solo, fully-booked business actually has to fix.

## Who genuinely benefits

The businesses that get real value tend to share one or more of these:

- **Calls arrive faster than one person can answer them** — multiple staff or vehicles out, or enough volume that some ring out every week.
- **A missed call has real weight.** If an average job is worth enough that losing a handful a year stings, the maths shifts quickly.
- **After-hours matters in your trade.** A burst pipe or a fault at 9pm does not wait, and whoever answers first usually gets it.
- **You're growing but not ready to hire admin.** This sits in the gap between doing it all yourself and taking someone on.
- **You keep noticing the same pattern** — quotes and calls slipping because nobody was free at the time.

## The alternatives, fairly

An answering service is not the only option and it is not always the next step.

**Voicemail with a good greeting.** Free, and often underused. The catch: most callers who reach voicemail hang up rather than leave a message, so it mainly helps the ones who were going to leave one anyway. Worth fixing regardless of what else you do.

**A partner or family member answering.** Free or close to it, and personal — customers like speaking to someone who clearly knows the business. The catch: it depends on their availability and goodwill, it bleeds into evenings in a way that causes friction at home, and it does not scale.

**A part-time admin.** Useful well beyond the phone — invoicing, scheduling, quotes — and a real step up in how organised things feel. The catch: real wages, time to hire and train, no after-hours unless you pay for it, and you are exposed when they are sick or on leave. [The full cost comparison](/guides/receptionist-or-outsource/) has the numbers.

**An answering service.** A third party takes the call so it does not ring out. Published New Zealand plans start around $43 a month for basic handling and run to a few hundred for higher-volume flat-rate cover. The catch: an operator who does not necessarily know your trade well enough to ask the right questions or judge what is genuinely urgent.

**A managed front desk.** Goes further than answering — captures the enquiry, replies fast, asks your qualifying questions, and follows up rather than stopping at "message taken". Costs more than voicemail, generally less than hiring, and sits alongside job software you already use. Ours starts from $1,500 setup plus $497 a month, in New Zealand dollars. ## Questions to ask before spending anything

- How many enquiries do I actually miss in an average week — not a bad week, an average one?
- What is a lost job really worth? Not just that job, but the repeat work and referrals that might have come with it.
- Am I fixing "I'm too busy right now", or am I trying to grow? Those need different answers.
- Would a better voicemail greeting and a genuine habit of ringing back within the hour fix most of this for free?
- Do I already have someone who could do this well if the process around it were clearer?
- Am I considering this because of one specific job I lost, or because it feels like something I should have?

If most of your answers point to "not really losing much", that is your answer. It is a perfectly good one.
