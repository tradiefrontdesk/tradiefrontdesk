## Nobody clicks an ad and books a renovation

A homeowner with a blocked drain rings three plumbers in the space of ten minutes and hires whoever answers first. A homeowner thinking about a kitchen renovation does nothing like that. They see an ad, maybe save it, maybe show a partner, maybe forget about it for a fortnight while life gets in the way, and eventually — if the idea survives that long — they get in touch. The click is not the start of a purchase. It is the start of a conversation that has not properly begun.

That difference matters more than it looks, because most of what gets written about paid ads assumes the plumber's version: urgency, comparison shopping, a decision made in minutes. None of it holds for building. A renovation or a new build is one of the largest sums most people will ever spend on their home, and nobody treats a decision that size the way they treat a blocked drain.

## What a building enquiry from an ad actually looks like

The person who fills in your form after seeing a set of before-and-after photos in their feed is often only exploring. They might have a rough idea — a kitchen that needs redoing, a house that has outgrown a young family — with no fixed budget and no clear timeline. That is not a low-quality enquiry. It is an early-stage one, and treating it as a lost cause because it did not turn into a booking this week misreads what it was.

The person who searches "builder [suburb]" and clicks a search ad is usually further along. They have already decided to build or renovate and are now working out who to trust with it, which means they arrive closer to ready than the person who was scrolling. Both are real enquiries. They are just at different points in a decision that, either way, still has months left to run — through a budget conversation, very possibly a partner who has not seen the numbers yet, a bank, a designer, and the ordinary back-and-forth of a large decision nobody rushes.

That is the thing to keep in mind whenever the job of a building ad gets judged. A tradie measuring a plumbing campaign can look at bookings made this month and get a fair read on whether it worked. A builder doing the same thing with a renovation campaign will almost always conclude it failed, because the decision it started has not finished. The click did its job the moment it produced a real conversation. Everything that turns that conversation into a signed contract happens afterwards, on the customer's timeline rather than the campaign's.

## Which channel tends to fit, and why

There is a reasonable case for both, and it is worth working through rather than picking one on instinct.

Search suits someone who already has a problem to solve now and is actively looking for who solves it — which describes the homeowner who has decided to renovate and is typing exactly that into Google. That intent is close to ready, and it tends to cost more per click precisely because everyone chasing that customer knows it.

Meta suits interrupting someone who was not looking yet, and building work is genuinely suited to that: a finished kitchen, a striking reclad, a well-lit new build are the kind of images that make someone stop scrolling and start thinking about their own house, before they had consciously decided to do anything about it. That is a real advantage for a trade whose output is visual and aspirational in a way a rewired switchboard is not.

Neither is automatically the better spend. A search enquiry usually needs less warming up but costs more to generate. A Meta enquiry usually needs longer to come to anything but can reach someone before a competitor even knows they exist. What [paid campaigns](/campaigns/) actually looks like in practice is often a mix of both, weighted toward whichever a given building business's work and area suit better — which is exactly the kind of judgement worth making at an audit rather than guessing at from a blog post.

## The qualification problem is worse here than anywhere

Every trade has to sort real enquiries from window-shoppers. Building has it worse, because the gap between what people imagine a renovation costs and what it actually costs is often enormous, and an ad does nothing to close that gap before the enquiry lands in your inbox.

A quick repair quote costs you an hour if it goes nowhere. A building quote does not. It is a site visit, a proper measure-up, calls to two or three subbies for pricing, and an evening spent actually writing the thing up — a full day of unpaid work, easily, before you know whether the budget behind the enquiry was ever close to the number you were about to produce. A builder who quotes everything a campaign sends through will burn real days on work that was never going to happen, and that time is a genuine cost of advertising, not a separate problem from it. It belongs in the same maths as the ad spend itself.

[Qualifying enquiries](/qualifying-enquiries/) before you commit to a measure-up is the fix, and it matters more on a paid enquiry than a free one, because you paid to generate the chance to ask. A short conversation about rough budget and scope before booking a site visit will not catch every mismatch, but it catches enough of them to protect the days that a full quote actually costs.

## Speed still matters — just not for the reason you would think

In an emergency trade, speed decides who answers the phone first while the customer is still ringing around. Building enquiries do not usually work that way — nobody is racing to lock in a builder in the next ten minutes. So why does the reply still matter?

Because it is the first evidence the customer gets about you. Someone weighing up a renovation is trying to work out, long before they sign anything, whether the person they are about to hand a large sum to is organised, responsive and easy to deal with. A prompt, useful first reply is a small proof of exactly that. A reply that takes a week, or never comes, is proof of the opposite — and it costs you the enquiry before the real decision has even begun, not because the customer was in a hurry, but because you already failed the one test you could control.

Being the builder who replied properly, and then stayed in touch without hassling anyone while the decision worked its way through a budget conversation, a partner, and everyone else involved, is what actually wins work that takes months to close. [Following up on building quotes](/guides/following-up-building-quotes/) covers how that ongoing contact works once a quote is out; the reply that opens the relationship is what earns you the chance to have that longer conversation at all.

## The gate

None of this changes the order things happen in. We do not run campaigns for a building business until the front desk that answers them is already in place — every enquiry replied to promptly, every one qualified before a day gets spent quoting it, and every quote followed up on a schedule rather than left to memory over a decision that might run for months. Paying to generate more enquiries into a business that cannot yet do that just multiplies the leak. [What running campaigns actually includes](/campaigns/), and the order we insist on, is laid out in full there.

That front desk is not a generic add-on either. [What it looks like for a building business](/builders/) is built around the shape of this trade specifically — renovation and new-build enquiries captured and qualified early, measure-ups booked against time you have actually made available, and quotes kept visible and followed up long after the first conversation goes quiet. Campaigns sit on top of that, not instead of it. A business without it is not ready to add paid enquiries to the pile, whatever the ad platform promises.

## Timing and seasonality

Building does not have the storm-driven spikes some trades see. What it does have is a rhythm tied to how people plan large decisions: renovation interest tends to lift over summer, when people are home, entertaining, and looking hard at a kitchen or outdoor space that is not quite working; enquiries for exterior work and recladding often pick up ahead of winter, while there is still time to get it done before the weather turns; and a new-build enquiry that starts in one season is frequently still working through finance and design several seasons later. None of that is a reason to time a campaign around a single peak the way a storm-chasing trade might. It is a reason to expect the enquiry you generate today to still be deciding well after the season that produced it has passed — which is exactly why the follow-up over that stretch, not the click that started it, is where a building campaign is actually won or lost.
