Heat pump work does not warm up gradually. Nobody is thinking about heating in March. Then the temperature drops hard on a Tuesday night in May or June, and by Thursday every installer in the district has a phone that will not stop ringing — the same enquiry, from a large share of the same suburb, inside the same few days. That is not bad luck or bad marketing. It is the shape of the trade. Two separate problems come out of it, and they are not the same problem: getting through the wall of enquiries without losing the ones you cannot serve this week, and doing something useful with the quiet stretch either side of it.

## The week everything changes

Most people do not decide gradually that they need a heat pump. They notice the house is cold, put it off, and keep putting it off, because a mild autumn evening does not create urgency. What creates urgency is a specific cold night — the kind where the family actually feels it, or an existing unit that has been limping along finally gives up entirely. That threshold gets crossed by a lot of households on roughly the same night, because they are all living under the same weather.

The result is not steady winter demand. It is a spike that arrives with almost no warning, on top of a workload that was already full from the installs booked earlier in the season. An installer who was comfortably busy on Monday can be three weeks deep in enquiries by Friday, without having done anything differently.

## "Not for three weeks" is the honest answer

Once the wall of calls lands, the true answer to most of them is not good news. You are booked. The earliest you can look at a new install is weeks away, and if it is a breakdown rather than a new install, even a callout might not happen today. That is not a failure on your part — it is what being in demand during peak season actually looks like — but it is not what the caller wants to hear, and it is rarely enough on its own to keep them waiting.

A caller with a cold house is not, in the moment, weighing up who does the tidiest install or who has the better reviews. They are working down a list, and the installer who answers with something concrete — a real date, a real next step — tends to keep the enquiry, even when the news is a wait. The installer who does not answer, or answers vaguely, loses it to whoever picks up next. In that sense, an enquiry you were too flat out to get to properly this week is doing the same damage as a call that rang out. The caller cannot tell the difference between "we're slammed and I haven't got to you yet" and "we didn't get your call" — both look identical from their end, which is exactly the problem [missed calls](/missed-calls/) covers in general. During a heat pump rush it happens far more often than usual, simply because there are more calls arriving than there is time to return them.

## What actually protects the enquiries you cannot take this week

The instinct in a genuine rush is to deal with whoever is in front of you and let the phone catch up later. That is understandable, and it is also how peak-season enquiries quietly disappear — not because anyone said no, but because nobody wrote them down before the next job started.

Two things matter more than anything else here. The first is capturing every enquiry somewhere you will actually look at again — name, number, what they need, and roughly how urgent it is — rather than trusting a missed-call list or your memory to hold it until you have a spare afternoon. The second is sorting urgency before you start calling people back, because not every call in the pile deserves the same priority. A household with no working heating at all in a cold snap, particularly if there is anyone elderly, unwell, or with young kids in the house, is a different situation to someone comparing quotes for a new install who can genuinely wait a fortnight. Working through the pile in the order it arrived, rather than by what actually needs you first, is how a real emergency ends up sitting behind three enquiries that could have waited without harm.

None of that needs to be elaborate. A shared list with those four details against each name does the job. What it protects against is the real failure mode of peak season: winnable jobs lost not to a competitor's better price, but to nobody having a record of them by the time there was time to call back.

## The trough is not empty, it is just unworked

Then the cold snap passes, or the season turns, and the phone goes quiet. This is where a lot of installers assume the rush is simply over for the year and move on to whatever is next. It is worth resisting that assumption, because the quiet stretch is not actually empty of work — it is full of the enquiries that arrived during the peak and never got a proper answer.

Someone who rang in the first cold week wanting a new install, and was told it would be three or four weeks out, has not necessarily gone elsewhere. Some will have. Plenty will still want the work done and are simply waiting to hear back, because that is what they were told would happen. The list built during the busy weeks — the one with names, jobs, and rough timeframes on it — is not a record of enquiries you failed to serve. It is next month's diary, sitting there unbooked.

## Turning the list into a schedule

Working that list is ordinary follow-up, not a special skill: call back in roughly the order the enquiries came in, confirm the job is still wanted, and offer an actual date now that the diary has space. Some will have sorted it elsewhere in the meantime, and that is a normal part of the process, not a sign the list was worthless. Enough of them will still be waiting to make working through it worth the time, and a quiet week is exactly when that time exists.

The mechanics of chasing an enquiry that has gone quiet — how soon, how many attempts, what to say — are the same regardless of trade, and [the guide to handling trade enquiries](/guides/handling-trade-enquiries/) covers that properly rather than repeating it here. What is specific to heat pump work is the shape of the opportunity: a genuinely slow week is not dead time to fill with other tasks. It is the appointment to finally return the calls the peak did not leave room for.

## The bottom line

Heat pump demand is never going to arrive evenly across the year, and no amount of planning changes that. What is within your control is what happens to an enquiry on either side of the spike: whether it gets written down and given an honest answer during the rush, and whether it gets picked back up once the rush is over rather than left to go cold in a call log nobody reopens. Handled that way, the peak stops being just a wall of calls to survive, and the trough stops being empty — each one is doing useful work for the other.
