A plumbing quote gets answered in a day or two, or it does not get answered at all. A building quote does not work like that, and treating it as if it does is how good jobs get written off early or chased into the ground.

## The quote itself is not a quick thing to produce

A repair quote is a number you can work out in your head on the way back to the ute. A renovation or new-build quote is not. It is a site visit, a proper measure-up, calls to two or three subbies for pricing, a check on material costs and lead times, working out how the job stages, and then an evening or a rained-out day actually writing the thing up properly. For a decent-sized reno or a new build, that is easily a full day of unpaid work before you have won anything. [Capturing the enquiry properly in the first place](/builders/) is what earns you the right to spend that day on it — the quote itself is the expensive part of the process, and it is worth protecting once it is out the door.

That is the detail that makes building different from most other trades. When a $400 callout quote goes cold, you have lost an hour. When an $80,000 reno quote goes cold, you have lost a day of skilled work, the diesel and the site visit, and quite possibly a start slot you kept clear in your calendar for it.

## Why the silence is longer, and why that is normal

A dripping tap does not need a bank's permission to get fixed. A kitchen extension or a new build usually does. Somewhere behind the silence on a big quote there is often a finance application working its way through a bank, a partner who has not seen the numbers yet, a designer or architect still finalising drawings, or a customer who simply needs a few weeks to get their head around a number that size before they can commit to it.

None of that is about you or your price. It is the shape of a large decision, and it runs on its own clock. A quick job gets decided in days because there is not much at stake and not much to arrange. A build gets decided in weeks, sometimes longer, because there genuinely is.

## The habit this creates, and how it goes wrong

Because six weeks of silence on a big quote is normal, builders learn — correctly — not to chase every job the moment it goes quiet. That instinct is right. The problem is it has no natural stopping point. The same builder who rightly does not chase in week one often never circles back at all, and the quote that took a full day to put together dies not because the customer said no, but because nobody checked in before the customer's attention moved somewhere else. [Why customers go quiet](/guides/why-customers-go-quiet/) covers the general reasons behind silence on any quote — busy, waiting on someone, comparing, forgotten, or genuinely out. On a build, the "waiting on someone" reason is doing most of the work, and it is worth taking seriously rather than assuming the worst.

## Telling still deciding from already gone

This is the actual problem on a job this size: the silence looks identical whether the customer is waiting on their bank or has already signed with someone else. A few things are worth reading, though none of them are certain.

A customer who engaged with the detail — asked about staging, queried a material choice, mentioned talking to the bank — is usually still in the process, even weeks later. A customer who went quiet the moment the number arrived and never asked a single follow-up question is a different case. And the clearest signal of all is not silence itself but a change in it: they stop answering on every channel at once, not just the quote thread, or you drive past the site and there is a skip bin and a different company's ute in the driveway. Silence on its own, on a job this size, is not that signal. Three weeks of nothing on a reno quote is not remotely unusual.

## Following up on a number this big without sounding like a hassle

A text that says "still keen to go ahead?" is fine for a $600 repair. On an $80,000 or $250,000 decision it can land as tone-deaf, because it treats a genuinely difficult decision like a small one. What works better is following up on the thing you actually know is holding it up, rather than the quote in general — "how did the finance conversation go" reads as someone who understands what they are waiting on, not someone chasing a sale. The mechanics of timing, wording and knowing when to stop are the same underlying skill whatever the job size, and [quote follow-up for tradies](/quote-follow-up/) covers that properly rather than repeating it here. The building-specific adjustment is simply to stretch the gaps out — a check-in at one week, another at three, and a final one closer to six than three, because that is closer to how long these decisions actually take.

## When to let a build quote go

Not every quiet build quote comes back, and chasing one for months past a clear decline helps nobody. The honest signal is usually a direct answer once you ask — most people, even ones who have gone elsewhere, will tell you if you ask plainly rather than vaguely — or the site activity mentioned above. Once you have that, log it and move your attention to the jobs still genuinely live. A graceful final message costs you nothing and keeps you in mind for the next job, which on a build-sized relationship is often worth more than this one was.
