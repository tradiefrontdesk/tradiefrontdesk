## The enquiry you sought out, and the one that found you

An organic enquiry has a history behind it. Someone had a job that needed doing, searched for a tradie in their area, looked at a few sites, maybe read a page or two, and then decided to get in touch. By the time their message lands in your phone, they have already done some choosing. They know roughly who you are.

A paid enquiry does not carry that history. It came from an ad running in a Facebook or Instagram feed, or a Google search result, and the person filling in the form was doing something else a moment before - scrolling, checking a message, looking at something unrelated - when your ad caught their attention. They stopped, filled in a name and a number, and kept scrolling. The intent behind that click is real. It is also shallow, and it is perishable in a way an organic enquiry is not.

This matters for how you treat the two kinds of enquiry, and it is a genuinely different argument from the general advice to "reply fast", which applies to both. The case for treating a paid enquiry as more urgent is specific to where it came from and what you paid to get it.

## An interrupted decision, not a considered one

Someone who searches "electrician [suburb]" has already decided they need an electrician and are actively choosing one. Someone who fills in a form on a paid ad has often made a much smaller decision: this ad looked relevant, so I will leave my number and see what happens. It is a lighter commitment, made faster, with less thought behind it.

That lighter commitment fades quickly. Within an hour, some of these people will genuinely struggle to remember which business they contacted, because they were never reading your site closely to begin with - they saw an ad, tapped it, and moved on with their day. A reply that arrives the next morning is not landing with someone who has been anxiously waiting to hear from a tradie they chose. It is landing with someone who has half-forgotten the ad ever happened, and who may need reminding what it was even about.

That is a different job for your reply to do than answering an organic enquiry. It has to reconnect the customer to a decision they made quickly and are not actively holding onto, before that connection fades entirely. [How fast do you actually need to reply to an enquiry?](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) covers the general reply-speed question in full; the case here is that a paid enquiry sits at the sharper, more time-sensitive end of it.

The best-known research on reply speed - the Lead Response Management Study, run out of MIT Sloan in 2007 - found a sharp drop-off in the odds of making contact once a lead sat for longer than a few minutes. It is worth knowing the study exists, but it studied inside sales teams cold-calling web leads, not trade businesses and not paid ad enquiries specifically, and it is nearly twenty years old. Treat it as one data point suggesting the general direction is real, not as a number to build a promise around. The fuller discussion of what that study does and does not support sits on the [reply-speed guide linked above](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) rather than here.

## The same auction is showing your competitors too

There is a second reason paid enquiries are more exposed than organic ones, and it has nothing to do with the customer's memory. While your ad reached them, so did several others. The same auction that served your ad to that person on that day is very likely serving similar ads to the same audience - other trades in the same category, in the same area, bidding on the same kind of customer. An organic search still has competing results on the page, but a paid enquiry sits inside a system explicitly designed to put multiple businesses in front of the same person around the same time.

That does not mean every paid lead is actively comparing three quotes before they have even spoken to anyone. Often they are not thinking about competitors at all - they filled in one form and moved on. But it does mean the field is more crowded than it looks, and whoever replies first has a real structural advantage that has nothing to do with who does better work. First contact tends to end the search before it properly starts, because most people stop looking the moment someone competent answers.

## You already paid for this one

Here is the part that makes a paid enquiry a different category of mistake to leave sitting. An organic enquiry that goes unanswered is a missed opportunity - frustrating, but it did not cost anything extra beyond the missed job itself. A paid enquiry that goes unanswered is an opportunity you paid for and then did not use. The ad spend that put your business in front of that person is already spent whether or not you ever reply. A slow reply on a paid lead is not just a lost job. It is a lost job you were invoiced for.

Think of it as buying something and then leaving it in the car park. The purchase happened. The value only gets realised if you actually go and collect it, and every hour it sits there is an hour closer to it being gone for good. Every minute of delay on a campaign enquiry is spending money to acquire something and then failing to use it - which is a worse outcome than never having spent the money at all, because at least then you would still have the cash. ## The volume problem: arithmetic, not laziness

There is a practical reason paid enquiries get missed that has nothing to do with anyone slacking off, and it is worth naming directly because it is so often mistaken for a discipline problem. Campaigns do not deliver enquiries evenly. A quiet week can be followed by a single good ad day that brings in more enquiries in an afternoon than the business would normally see in a week - a change in the ad, an audience that responded well, a competitor pausing their own campaign, or simply a run of good weather that got more people thinking about their gutters or their switchboard.

A tradie who can comfortably manage two or three enquiries a day cannot suddenly manage twelve, no matter how well-intentioned they are about replying fast. Something has to give, and what usually gives is exactly the enquiries that arrived last - the ones sitting at the bottom of a pile that only existed because the campaign worked. That is not a person being lazy or careless. It is arithmetic: a fixed amount of attention meeting a variable, sometimes spiky, amount of demand. The busiest day for a campaign is precisely the day a normal reply process is least able to keep up, which is the opposite of what a business paying for that campaign needs to happen.

It is also the day the cost of the failure is highest, because every one of those enquiries was paid for individually. A quiet week where a couple of organic enquiries slip through is a bad week. A good ad day where half a dozen paid enquiries go unanswered because they all landed within an hour of each other is a bad week and a wasted ad budget stacked on top of it. The two problems compound each other precisely when the business can least afford it, which is the moment demand and capacity are furthest apart.

This is also why missed calls specifically deserve their own attention on a campaign-heavy day - a burst of enquiries arriving while you are already on the tools is exactly the scenario [missed-call cover](/missed-calls/) exists for, whether those calls came from an ad, a search, or a customer who already knew your name.

## What this means in practice

None of this is an argument for panic, and it is not a promise that any particular reply time will win any particular job. It is an argument for treating a paid enquiry as time-sensitive in a specific way that an organic one usually is not: the interest behind it is real but fades fast, the same platform is very possibly showing your competitors the same opportunity, the money is already spent regardless of what happens next, and the enquiries themselves tend to arrive in bursts precisely when you have the least capacity to deal with them.

Put together, that is the case for making sure every paid enquiry gets a reply without depending on someone noticing it in time between jobs.
