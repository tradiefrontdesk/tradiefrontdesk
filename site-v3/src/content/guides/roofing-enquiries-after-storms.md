Bad weather does not send roofers a steady trickle of new work. It sends a spike. A run of a few bad days can generate a week's worth of enquiries compressed into hours, all from people who want an answer now, not from people prepared to wait their turn in a queue.

## The enquiry does not look like your normal enquiry

A person calling about a leak is not calling the way someone calls about a reroof quote in April. They are often standing under a bucket, or have just found a stain spreading across a ceiling, and they want to know two things: can you come, and how soon. Some of them are also fielding a call from an insurer at the same time, which adds another party expecting a fast, clear answer from you before anything gets booked.

That combination — urgency plus an audience of one who is stressed and possibly juggling someone else's process too — means these callers do not behave like a normal quote enquiry. They do not leave a message and wait patiently for a callback tomorrow. Many of them are working down a list, calling the next roofer the moment the first one does not pick up, because a leaking roof does not pause while you finish what you're doing.

## The cruel timing

Here is the part that makes this specifically a roofer's problem, not a general enquiry-handling problem. The week that produces the most enquiries is the same week you have the least capacity to deal with them.

Bad weather does not just generate phone calls. It generates work — temporary patches, tarping, emergency callouts on properties already on your books, plus every new enquiry stacking up behind them. You are more flat out than usual at exactly the moment more people than usual are trying to reach you. On an ordinary week, a missed call might sit for an hour before you notice it. In a storm week, missed calls can queue up for most of a day while you are up a ladder dealing with the last one.

That is the trap. It is not that you are bad at answering the phone. It is that the mechanism which normally lets you catch up — a quiet stretch between jobs, a lunch break, the drive home — disappears in exactly the week you need it most.

## Why speed matters more than usual here

On a quiet week, a slow reply costs you some of the edge but the customer is often still deciding, still comparing, still working through a normal timeline. During a weather spike that patience is gone. Someone with water coming through their ceiling is not weighing up five quotes over a fortnight — they are trying to get someone, anyone, out to look at it, and the first roofer who responds with a real answer tends to get the inspection booked.

That is not really about being the best roofer in the area that week. It is about being the one who replied. [How fast should tradies reply to enquiries](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) covers the general shape of that problem — what counts as fast enough, and why a rough same-day rule beats trying to be glued to your phone. During a genuine weather spike, treat that guidance as a floor, not a target: anything urgent-sounding deserves the fastest response you can manage, even if it is only a short message confirming you got it and you will call back.

## What tends to fall through in a spike specifically

The failure mode in a normal week and the failure mode in a storm week are different, and it is worth knowing which one you are dealing with.

In a normal week, a slow reply usually just means the enquiry drifts and eventually goes cold. In a storm week, the more common failure is that the enquiry gets lost entirely. Your phone is ringing while you are on a roof with wet hands and no way to answer it. A missed call notification arrives alongside four others. By the time you are back in the van, it is one line in a list, indistinguishable from the others, and the person on the other end has already rung the next name down. [Missed-call cover](/missed-calls/) is built for exactly this — catching what arrives while your hands are genuinely full, rather than relying on you noticing a missed call between jobs on the busiest week of the year.

The other thing that tends to break down is triage. On a normal day you can tell the difference between someone chasing a quote and someone with an active leak, because there is time to think about it. In a storm week, everything arrives urgent-sounding and it is hard to tell, from a missed call alone, who genuinely needs you today versus who can reasonably wait until next week. Getting that sorted before you call people back — rather than working through a flat list in the order it arrived — is what stops a genuine emergency sitting behind three enquiries that could have waited.

## What this actually means for you

You cannot flatten the spike. Weather is going to keep doing what weather does, and there is no version of running a roofing business where the enquiries after bad weather arrive at a steady, manageable pace instead of all at once. What you can change is what happens to those enquiries while you are up on a roof and cannot get to your phone.

The [roofers page](/roofers/) sets out the wider shape of that — capturing enquiries, replying fast, and following up on the inspections and quotes that come out of a spike like this. The specific point worth taking from this guide is narrower: the busiest week for roofing enquiries is also the week you are least available, and that mismatch is the actual problem, not a lack of demand or a lack of skill. Fixing it is less about answering every call yourself and more about making sure nothing goes quiet while your hands are full — a holding reply, a way of catching what you miss, and a clear sense of who needs you today versus who can wait until the ladder comes down.
