There is no single right answer to "how should my phone get answered." There are four real options, and each one is genuinely the best choice for somebody — just not for everybody. This is a straight comparison, including the parts where the option we sell is not the one you should pick.

## A pure AI receptionist or AI answering app

This is software, usually a monthly subscription, that answers calls or messages, holds a basic conversation, and takes down what the caller needs. Plenty of these products now exist, and at their best they are genuinely good at the narrow job they do: a call rings out, the app answers instead of voicemail, and a set of details comes through instead of nothing.

The appeal is obvious. It is cheap next to a person, it is instant to switch on, and it never sleeps — a roofer's phone rings at 9pm after a storm and something answers it, every time, without anyone paying overtime.

The weaknesses are just as real, and worth saying plainly. It is a tool, not a service — you are the one who has to write the greeting, decide what questions get asked, and keep it updated when your prices or your service area change. It does not know your business unless you teach it, and most owners buying it are already flat out on the tools, which is exactly why the teaching often does not happen properly. And once it has taken the details, its job is largely done. If a customer goes quiet after getting a quote, nothing in the app notices or follows up — that part is still on you, same as if nobody had answered the phone at all.

For a business that mainly needs calls caught and details taken, and is happy to configure and maintain the tool itself, this can be a perfectly good answer.

## A traditional human answering service

This is the older model: a call centre or answering service takes your calls, usually during business hours or around the clock depending on what you pay for, and a real person on the other end takes a message or books a callback.

The strength here is reassurance. Some customers — an older homeowner with a burst pipe, someone anxious about a big renovation spend — settle the moment they hear a real voice rather than a recording or a script that feels automatic. That matters more for some trades and some customers than others.

The weaknesses tend to be cost and fit. Pricing is commonly per-call or per-minute, so a busy week costs more than a quiet one, which makes the bill hard to predict. The person answering is usually working from a generic script across many different businesses, not specifically trained on your trade — they may not know that a "no hot water" call from an elderly customer in winter needs to jump the queue, or that "smell of gas" is not a message to take calmly and pass on later. And like the AI option, the job usually stops at taking a message. Nobody on that end is chasing your quote a week later to see if the customer is still deciding.

## Hiring someone part-time

If you can afford it and can keep them busy, this is genuinely the best quality option on the table. A person who works for your business, learns your regulars, knows which supplier to call and which customers pay late, and can do far more than answer the phone — quoting, invoicing, scheduling the crew — earns their keep doing all of it, not just the calls. [The full cost comparison](/guides/receptionist-or-outsource/) walks through what a hire actually costs once KiwiSaver, ACC and leave are counted, and when it is worth it.

The weaknesses are the ones every small employer already knows. Recruiting takes time and sometimes an agency fee. Wages, once you add the on-costs, run well past the advertised hourly rate. They get sick, they take four weeks of annual leave, and somebody still has to answer the phone while they are away. And however good they are, they cannot answer at 9pm on a Tuesday unless you are paying for a shift that late — which almost nobody is, for a role that spends most evenings quiet.

## A managed front desk — what we do

This is a different shape again, not a bigger version of the AI receptionist. Software handles the first reply, so it happens immediately, at any hour, without waiting for someone to be free. But the reply is not improvised — it runs from wording the business owner has approved, it does not invent prices or make promises the owner has not signed off on, and anything it genuinely cannot handle goes to a person. The part that makes it a managed front desk rather than a tool is that the whole process — capturing the enquiry, asking the right qualifying questions for that trade, booking the customer in, and following up if a quote goes quiet — is set up and run for the business, not handed over as software to configure.

Said plainly, the trade-off: this costs more than an app. It is more than a very small operator needs if they are already answering their own phone reliably and are not trying to grow. And it is not instant — getting the wording, the qualifying questions and the escalation rules right takes real setup, not a five-minute signup. [What's included](/whats-included/) sets out exactly what that setup covers plan by plan. None of that makes it the right fit for everyone reading this, and it is worth saying so rather than pretending otherwise.

Where it earns its cost is the businesses missing enough enquiries, after-hours calls, or dead quotes that the gap is genuinely costing paid work — and who would rather have that whole process handled than build and maintain another piece of software themselves.

## Will the customer be able to tell, and does it matter?

This is the question most tradies actually have, underneath all the comparison shopping: will my customer know, and will it put them off?

Sometimes they can tell. Sometimes they cannot. What the evidence of how people actually behave suggests matters far more to a customer than who or what typed the reply is whether the answer was fast, accurate, and useful. Picture two versions of the same enquiry. In one, a customer texts about a leaking hot water cylinder at 8pm and gets an instant reply confirming someone will call first thing, with a rough idea of what the callout involves. In the other, the same customer rings three tradies, gets voicemail from two, and a slow, vague reply the next afternoon from the third. Nobody is choosing the second business because a human eventually typed the message. They are choosing whoever answered while the problem still felt urgent.

A slow human reply loses to a fast, clear one almost every time, because the customer's problem does not care who is on the other end — it cares whether it is being taken seriously right now. That is not a reason to hide what is happening. If a customer asks directly whether they are talking to a person, the honest answer is the only acceptable one, and pretending a human is typing live when software is doing the reply is not something any of these approaches should ever do. But the customer's actual priority, in the moment their phone is in their hand and the tap is dripping onto the floor, is getting looked after — not auditioning who is on the other end.

## So which one is right?

Start with your own volume and habits, not with whichever option has the best advertising. If you are answering your own phone reliably and are not trying to grow past what you personally handle, none of these four are worth paying for — a good voicemail greeting and a genuine callback habit will do most of the work for free.

If you mainly need calls caught and details taken, and you are happy to set the tool up yourself and keep it current, an AI receptionist app is a sensible, low-cost choice. If reassurance from a real voice matters more to your customers than cost predictability, a traditional answering service earns its higher price. If you have enough other admin work to fill a role and can commit to a steady wage bill, hiring is the best quality answer money can buy, short of covering the hours a person physically cannot work.

A managed front desk fits the businesses in between — enough enquiries, after-hours calls or slipping quotes that the gap is real, and a preference for the whole process being run rather than another login to maintain.
