Reply inside an hour if you can manage it, and never let a full day pass without at least an acknowledgement. That is the realistic target for a New Zealand trade business — not a guarantee that faster always wins the job. There is no verified New Zealand study putting an exact number on trade response times, and anyone quoting you one is guessing. What the closest real research shows is that speed matters more than most owners assume, and that most businesses are slower than they think.

## How fast is fast enough?

There is no magic number for trades. What holds up across everything worth reading is the shape of the problem: response time affects whether an enquiry becomes a real conversation, and the gap between fast and slow is wider than people expect. An hour is a good target. A day is the outer limit before an enquiry starts to feel ignored, even if the customer never says so.

What makes this trade-specific is the constraint, not the target. A call centre with people at desks hits "within minutes" routinely. A plumber under a house with wet hands cannot, and pretending otherwise just manufactures guilt about something that was never realistic.

## What actually happens while a customer waits

Nothing dramatic. They do not sit refreshing their phone. They get on with their day, and three things tend to happen while they do.

**They often keep looking.** A burst pipe or no hot water gets three or four calls out in short order, not one. Whoever answers first often has the job before the others ring back.

**The urgency shifts.** An emergency stays urgent. A renovation enquiry drifts down the list the longer it sits, and by the time you ring back they may have moved to "we'll sort it later".

**Silence becomes evidence.** Fair or not, a slow first reply reads as a preview of what it will be like to be your customer.

## What the research actually shows

The best evidence here is genuinely verifiable: Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington's "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads", published in *Harvard Business Review* in March 2011. The researchers audited 2,241 US companies and found an average first response of **42 hours** among firms that replied at all, and that **23% never replied**. Firms making contact within an hour were substantially more likely to qualify the enquiry than those waiting longer, and dramatically more likely than firms waiting a day or more.

Be precise about what that is and is not. It measured US business-to-business companies responding to web-form enquiries — not phone calls, not trade businesses, not New Zealand, and not this or any other product. What it credibly shows is that first responders win a disproportionate share, and that most businesses respond far slower than they assume. That principle travels even where the exact numbers do not.

You will also see the "five-minute rule" quoted around the internet, usually traced to a 2007 study. The idea — sooner beats later, and the gap matters most early — is worth taking seriously. The specific number is not something we will put our name behind: the original research is hard to verify independently and gets reported differently depending on who is citing it. Take "fast beats slow" as the lesson, not the minute count.

## Realistic targets when you're on the tools

- **Under an hour when you can.** Between jobs, on a break, waiting on a delivery — a fast reply costs nothing and puts you ahead of whoever else they are ringing.
- **Same day, always.** If it comes in first thing and you are flat out until knock-off, same-day contact is fair and achievable. Not "within the hour", but not "whenever I get to it" either.
- **Within minutes for anything urgent.** Burst pipe, no power, no hot water. These are the ones where the customer is actively working down a list.
- **A holding reply beats no reply.** "Got your message, will call you back by 3pm" changes how the wait feels and buys you time to deal with it properly.

None of this means guaranteeing a response time you cannot control. It means having a rule for yourself, and something to catch what falls through when you genuinely cannot get to the phone.

## How to hit these without living on your phone

The honest tension is that "reply fast" and "stay on the tools" pull against each other all day. A few things help.

**Set a rule and stick to it** — same-day for everything, immediate for urgent — rather than trying to be instantly available, which is not sustainable for someone doing physical work.

**Use a holding message for calls you cannot take.** Even a generic "on a job, will call back today" sent the moment you notice a missed call closes the gap between silence and an answer, and stops the customer ringing the next name purely because your phone rang out.

**Separate urgent from non-urgent before replying to either.** A renovation enquiry does not need to interrupt a job the way a burst pipe does. Treating them identically either makes you drop tools too often or makes real emergencies wait.

**Get something catching what you miss.** This is where a managed front desk earns its keep — an enquiry captured and acknowledged the moment it arrives, against your own rules about what is urgent, rather than sitting in a missed-calls list until you are back at the ute.
