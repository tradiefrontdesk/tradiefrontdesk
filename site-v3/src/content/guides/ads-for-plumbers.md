## Two businesses under one ad account

A plumbing business sells two different things, and they do not behave the same way in front of a paid campaign. One side is happening right now: a burst pipe at 6am, a blocked drain backing up into a laundry, a hot water cylinder that has failed the morning guests arrive. There is no calendar involved and no real decision-making — the customer has a problem on the floor and needs it stopped. The other side is planned: a bathroom renovation, a hot water system being replaced before the old one actually dies, a drainage upgrade scheduled around other trades. That customer has weeks, sometimes months, and is comparing more than one plumber before committing.

Run a campaign without separating those two in your own head, and you end up managing them as if they were one audience with one level of urgency. They are not, and the gap between them is wider for plumbing than for almost any other trade on this site — which is exactly why it needs its own answer rather than borrowing the argument written for paid enquiries generally.

## Why search carries more of the weight here

For the emergency side of the business, the customer is not being introduced to plumbing as an idea. They already have the problem, they already know they need a plumber, and they are actively looking for one right now — typing "plumber [suburb]" or "blocked drain [suburb]" into Google with water already on the floor. That is about as close to pure intent as paid advertising gets. Search puts your business in front of someone at the exact moment they are choosing, rather than trying to create interest that was not there a minute earlier.

Meta works differently, and it is not irrelevant here — it just fits a different part of the job list. Nobody scrolling Instagram at 9pm has a burst pipe on their mind, but plenty of them have a tired bathroom they have been meaning to do something about, and a well-shot before-and-after can put that thought in front of them before they would ever have gone looking for it themselves. That is the planned side of the business: renovations, upgrades, drainage work that improves rather than repairs. It rewards patience and a considered decision in a way the emergency side never will.

The practical read for a plumbing business is that search usually deserves more of the budget and more of the attention than it would for a trade whose work is mostly planned and visual. The urgent half of this trade is a textbook case of a customer who has already decided they need help and is choosing who answers first.

## The click that expires while you're still driving

This is the part specific to plumbing worth sitting with properly: a paid click in this trade has a use-by time measured in minutes, not hours. Someone standing over a laundry with water rising does not wait patiently for a callback. They ring, and if nothing happens fast, they ring the next name down the list — and they keep doing that until somebody picks up. The same enquiry that was worth a real job the moment it arrived can be worth nothing two hours later, because by then the water is off, the drain is cleared, and someone else has already been under the sink.

That is a different shape of urgency to almost every other trade covered on this site. A quiet quote for a bathroom reno can sit for a week and still be live. A paid emergency plumbing enquiry does not get that grace period. The ad spend that put your business in front of that person is already spent the instant they clicked, whether or not anyone ever calls them back — and for this trade specifically, the window in which that spend can still turn into a job is often shorter than the time it takes to finish the job you are currently on. [How fast you actually need to reply to an enquiry](/guides/how-fast-to-reply-to-enquiries/) covers the general case for speed properly; plumbing's emergency side is close to the sharpest version of that argument this site makes anywhere.

The planned work does not carry that same clock. A hot water replacement or a drainage upgrade enquiry from a campaign can sit for a day or two and still be worth calling — the customer made a considered decision to leave their details, not an impulsive one made under pressure. Treating every paid enquiry from this trade as equally perishable would be wrong in the other direction, chasing a renovation lead with the same panic a burst pipe deserves. The two need to be told apart the moment they arrive, not sorted out later.

## Buying disruption you can't schedule around

There is a second cost specific to this trade that has nothing to do with losing the enquiry itself, and it is worth naming plainly because campaigns can make it worse rather than better. Paid emergency enquiries arrive with no warning at all — not at a time you chose, not spaced out through a day you planned. A booked day of scheduled plumbing work — reroutes, installs, a bathroom fit-out — gets interrupted the moment a genuine emergency call comes in, because that is the nature of the work: it does not check your diary first.

Buying more of those emergency enquiries through a campaign, without a way to sort the genuinely urgent from the merely anxious-sounding, means paying money specifically to generate more disruption to work you had already sold. That is a worse outcome than a campaign that simply underperforms — it is a campaign that actively costs you on the job you already had booked, on top of whatever it cost to acquire the enquiry in the first place. [Triaging emergency plumbing calls](/guides/triaging-emergency-plumbing-calls/) goes into how that sorting actually works in practice — what a call needs to establish, and how to fit a genuine emergency into a day without letting it bulldoze the customer who booked three weeks ago. A campaign is only worth running once that sorting exists, because a campaign without it just increases the volume passing through a process that is already struggling to keep both sides of the business intact.

## Where the gate matters most

This is the reason we will not start campaign work for a plumbing business until the front desk is in place. Of all the trades on this site, plumbing has the least tolerance for a gap between an enquiry arriving and someone doing something with it — the emergency side collapses that gap to almost nothing, and the planned side still competes against however many other plumbers a customer is quietly comparing. Paying to generate more enquiries into a phone that goes to voicemail while you are on the tools does not create more business. It creates more evidence of the same leak, at your own expense, and on the exact urgent enquiries where the cost of missing one is highest.

The order we work in reflects that: [the front desk goes in first](/plumbers-drainage/), so that whatever a campaign brings in — a genuine leak call at 9pm or a bathroom enquiry on a Tuesday afternoon — gets captured, triaged and routed the same way every other enquiry to the business already is. Only once that exists does it make sense to talk about [paid campaigns](/campaigns/) at all. Search for the urgent work, a considered channel for the planned work, and a way to tell the two apart the second they land — that is what running ads for a plumbing business actually looks like, and none of it pays off without somewhere reliable for the enquiry to go.

## Seasonality worth planning around

Plumbing does have a genuine seasonal pattern, and it is sharper on the emergency side than the planned one. Cold snaps push a spike in burst pipes and failed hot water systems — pipes that have sat fine for years fail the first hard frost, and a household with no hot water treats that as an emergency regardless of the time of day. Storm events do something similar to blocked and overwhelmed drains. Both produce a short burst of urgent search demand that looks nothing like the steady trickle of renovation and installation enquiries running through the rest of the year.

That matters for how a campaign gets planned, not just how it gets answered. A search campaign that is switched on and budgeted for ahead of a forecast cold snap is catching demand that is about to spike anyway; the same campaign running flat through a mild week is competing for a smaller pool of genuinely urgent searches. The planned side of the business does not move with the weather in the same way, and campaigns aimed at it can run on a steadier, more predictable rhythm through the year. Recognising which kind of demand a campaign is chasing, and when it naturally rises and falls, is part of spending that budget on the enquiries most likely to still be there when you call back.
