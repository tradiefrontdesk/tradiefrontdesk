# Speed to lead — verified fact sheet

**Compiled 9 August 2026.** This is the ONLY permitted factual basis for the
speed-to-lead content. Do not add figures from memory, from other blogs, or
from sales-tool marketing pages.

## The one figure we are allowed to use

**Lead Response Management Study (2007).** Dr. James Oldroyd, MIT Sloan School
of Management, in partnership with InsideSales.com.

- Method: three years of data, six companies, **more than 15,000 leads** and
  **over 100,000 call attempts**.
- Finding, in the study's own terms: *the odds of contacting a lead if called
  in 5 minutes versus 30 minutes drop 100 times; the odds of qualifying a lead
  if called in 5 minutes versus 30 minutes drop 21 times.*
- Public copies: content.marketingsherpa.com (full report PDF) and
  onecavo.com (7-page executive summary of the 35-page study).

### State these caveats. Do not bury them.

1. **It is from 2007.** Nearly twenty years old. Phones, texting and customer
   expectations have all changed since. We cite it because it is the best
   methodologically-stated study on the subject, not because it is recent.
2. **It is not about tradies.** It studied business-to-business inside sales
   teams calling web leads. A plumbing business is not an inside sales floor.
   The direction of the finding travels; the exact multiples do not.
3. **InsideSales.com sold lead-response software.** They had a commercial
   interest in the result. The research lead was at MIT Sloan and the method
   is stated, which is why it clears our bar — but a reader deserves to know
   who paid for it.

Framed that way, the study is an honest input. Framed as "studies show you must
reply in 5 minutes or lose the job", it is the same kind of borrowed authority
this project has rejected six times.

## What we must NOT claim

**The "300% from speed to lead" figure does not hold up.** It was checked. The
300–400% figure that circulates attaches to Alex Hormozi's *value equation*
framework, not to speed to lead, and even there it appears only in third-party
write-ups with no original data behind it. Do not attribute it to Hormozi, do
not attach it to reply speed, do not use it at all.

**Do not cite Hormozi as evidence.** He is a marketer with an opinion, not a
study. If his framing is genuinely useful, the honest move is to make the
argument on its own merits, which is easy here because the argument is
self-evidently true to anyone who has ever rung three tradies and hired the one
who rang back.

**No other speed statistics.** Not "78% of customers buy from the first
responder", not "you are 7x more likely to qualify", not "50% of buyers choose
the vendor that responds first". These circulate endlessly and none of them
trace to a stated method. Already-rejected on this project: "62% of calls go
unanswered", "$100–200 per missed call", "80% of sales need 5 follow-ups".

## The argument that needs no statistic at all

This is the strongest material available and it costs nothing to verify,
because the reader has lived it:

A homeowner with a blocked drain rings three plumbers. The first one to pick up
or ring back gets the job — not because they are the best plumber, but because
the problem is urgent and the customer stops looking the moment someone
competent answers. The other two never find out they were in the running. They
do not experience it as losing a job; they experience it as a quiet phone.

That is the whole case. It does not need a percentage, and a reader recognises
it instantly.
